## Let It Snow, Anywhere

The snow screen turns any display into a window on a snowy day: flakes drifting down over a winter scene, each falling at its own speed and swaying slightly as it goes. It is made for the moments when a little winter atmosphere helps, whether a holiday party, a cozy evening, a shop window in December, or just a desk on a hot summer day.

1. Press <kbd>F</kbd> for fullscreen.
2. Press <kbd>Space</kbd> to open the controls.
3. Choose a background scene, a snowflake tint, and how much snow you want.
4. Turn on a soundtrack if you like, then press <kbd>Space</kbd> to hide the panel.

## Shaping the Snowfall

- **Snowflake Count:** from 20 flakes for a gentle flurry to 800 for a blizzard.
- **Image mode:** press <kbd>C</kbd> to swap soft round flakes for detailed six-pointed crystals. **Image Max Opacity** controls how solid the crystals are, and **Rotation Speed** how quickly they spin.
- **Color tint:** twenty tints from Pure Snow and Glacier Blue through Aurora Green, Lavender, and Ice Rose to Golden Amber and Warm Charcoal. White and pale blue look most natural; the bright tints suit parties and themed streams.
- **Background scene:** several winter photographs to set the snow against.

Press <kbd>M</kbd> to toggle the sound and <kbd>R</kbd> to return to the default settings.

## Ideas for Using It

- **Holiday TV:** a living-room TV showing snow with a soft soundtrack makes a festive backdrop for a Christmas or New Year's party.
- **Shop windows and lobbies:** a screen of falling snow draws the eye in a winter window display without any text.
- **Streams and videos:** a calm "be right back" scene for December streams, or a background for holiday greetings.
- **Kids' rooms:** a few slow, large crystals on a dim tablet are a gentle bedtime visual.
- **A cooler-feeling room:** on a summer afternoon, a snowy scene on a second monitor is a small, pleasant illusion.

## A Note on Snowflakes

Real snow crystals grow in a six-sided pattern because of the way water molecules lock together as ice. Their exact shape, from plates to branching stars to needles, depends on the temperature and humidity in the cloud where they form, which is why no two look quite alike. The crystal flakes in image mode follow that classic six-pointed form.
