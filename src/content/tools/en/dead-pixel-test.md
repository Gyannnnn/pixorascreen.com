## What Is a Dead Pixel?

A dead pixel is a single point on your display that no longer lights up at all. It receives no power, so it stays black no matter what the screen is showing — it does not flicker, change color, or respond to anything you do. Against a bright background it reads as a tiny dark speck that never moves, which is why a full white or light-colored screen is the fastest way to find one.

A **stuck pixel** is a different fault that is easy to confuse with it. A stuck pixel is still receiving power, but it is frozen on one color — usually red, green, or blue — so instead of a dark speck you get a small dot that glows constantly. The distinction matters because it changes what you can do about it: stuck pixels sometimes recover with rapid color cycling or very gentle pressure, while a genuinely dead pixel is a hardware fault that software cannot bring back.

Both are worth identifying precisely before you contact a manufacturer, because warranty policies are usually written around a minimum number of confirmed defective pixels, and they often treat dead and stuck pixels differently.

## Why Test With Six Colors?

A single color only tells you part of the story. Each screen in this test isolates a different sub-pixel or a different class of defect, and a fault that is obvious on one can be completely invisible on the others.

### Black reveals stuck-on and bright pixels

When the whole panel should be dark, any pixel that is still emitting light has nowhere to hide. This is the screen that catches stuck pixels and, on LCD panels, light leaking in from the edges.

### White reveals dead and dim pixels

With every sub-pixel driven to full brightness, a pixel that is off shows up as a dark dot. White is also the easiest screen for spotting dust, smudges, and fingerprints, which are frequently mistaken for pixel defects.

### Red, green, and blue isolate each sub-pixel

Every pixel on a color display is made of a red, a green, and a blue sub-pixel. Filling the screen with one pure channel lights only that sub-pixel across the entire panel, so a failure in just one channel becomes obvious. A pixel whose red sub-pixel has died still looks perfectly normal on the green and blue screens, and can pass unnoticed on white — which is exactly why these three screens are not optional.

### Grey reveals backlight unevenness

Mid-grey is the most revealing screen for panel uniformity. Pure white is bright enough to mask gradual variation, and pure black gives the backlight nothing to illuminate, but an even mid-tone makes clouding, patchiness, and brighter corners stand out. On an LCD or LED panel this is where clouding and uneven backlight are easiest to judge, while bleed at the edges shows best on black. On OLED there is no backlight to bleed, so an uneven grey points to worn pixels instead — the [OLED burn-in test](tool:oled-burn-in-test) is built for that case.

## How to Perform a Dead Pixel Test

The test itself takes about a minute, but a few conditions make the difference between a reliable result and a false alarm.

### 1. Clean the screen first

Wipe the display with a dry microfiber cloth before you start. A speck of dust on a high-density phone or laptop panel looks almost identical to a dead pixel, and this single step prevents most false positives.

### 2. Turn brightness to maximum

Dim or dying sub-pixels only separate from healthy ones at full output. Raise the display to 100% brightness and disable any adaptive brightness, night mode, or blue-light filter, since those shift color and can hide a faulty channel.

### 3. Work in a dim room

Ambient light and reflections wash out exactly the low-contrast detail you are looking for. A darkened room is essential for the black and blue screens in particular, where the defects you are hunting are faint by nature.

### 4. View from a few angles

Some defects, and most backlight bleeding, only appear off-axis. Check each color straight on first, then from the left, right, and slightly above. On a phone or tablet, tilt the device rather than moving yourself.

### 5. Cycle through all six colors slowly

Give each color ten to fifteen seconds and scan the panel in sections rather than staring at the middle. Check the corners and edges last — that is where bleeding and clouding concentrate. Turn on auto-advance if you would rather keep both hands free, or step through with the arrow keys and revisit any color from the quick-select row.

If you do find something, photograph it against the color that shows it most clearly. A dated photo of the defect is far more useful than a description when you open a warranty claim.
