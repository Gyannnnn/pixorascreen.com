## How to Use

The two modes sit behind the toggle on the left of the control bar, and switching between them clears whatever was running so you never inherit a half-finished count. **Timer** counts down from a duration you choose. **Stopwatch**counts up from zero and records laps.

### Setting a duration

The preset buttons cover the durations most people reach for: one minute, five, ten, fifteen, twenty five, and an hour. For anything else, type minutes and seconds into the two boxes and press Set. The display updates immediately so you can confirm the duration before you start it, which matters when you are setting up in front of an audience.

### The alarm

Two alarms are available from the dropdown next to the Alarm checkbox: a rising **chime** for quiet rooms and a repeating **beep** that cuts through noise. Both are generated in the browser, so neither adds a download. When the countdown reaches zero the entire screen also flashes, which is the part that carries across a room when a device is muted or the audience is loud. Uncheck Alarm for a silent finish with the flash alone.

### Running it fullscreen

Press <kbd>F</kbd> and the digits scale to fill the display, which is what the page is built around. <kbd>Space</kbd> starts and pauses,<kbd>R</kbd> resets,<kbd>L</kbd> records a lap, and<kbd>M</kbd> swaps modes, so you can run a whole session without touching the controls. The dark and light themes suit a dimmed room and a bright one respectively.

## Timer vs. Stopwatch: Which Do I Need?

The distinction is simply which end of the interval you care about. Use a **timer** when the length is decided in advance and the thing that matters is being told when it is over: a ten minute talk, a thirty minute exam, eight minutes for the pasta. You set the number, and the alarm does the watching so you do not have to.

Use a **stopwatch** when the length is the thing you are trying to find out. It starts at zero and keeps going until you stop it, which is what you want for timing how long a task actually takes, running drills, or measuring repeated attempts. Laps matter here: they let you mark points along the way and compare the splits between them afterwards.

A quick test if you are unsure: if you can say the number before you begin, you want the timer. If the number is the answer, you want the stopwatch.

## Common Uses

### Presentations and conference talks

Set the slot length, put it fullscreen on a laptop facing the speaker or a monitor at the back of the room, and nobody has to make wind-up gestures. The large digits are the whole point here; a phone timer on a lectern is unreadable from three metres away.

### Classroom activities and exams

A shared countdown on the projector answers the how-long-left question for the whole room at once. The flash at zero is easier to respond to than a sound in a busy classroom, and it works with the projector audio muted.

### Workshops and timed exercises

Short rounds are exactly what the presets are for: start a five or ten minute block, reset between them, and keep the same screen up all session. Stopwatch mode with laps suits exercises where you want to record how long each group actually needed.

### Cooking

A tablet propped on the counter with a fullscreen countdown is readable from across the kitchen with your hands full, and the custom entry handles the odd durations recipes ask for.

### Workouts and interval training

Time work and rest intervals from a phone or a TV, and use the stopwatch with laps to log rounds or sets. The beep alarm is the one to pick here, since it carries over music and effort.

### Focus sessions

A twenty five minute preset is there for exactly this. That page is the better home for a repeating focus cycle; this one is better when you just need a clock counting down.
