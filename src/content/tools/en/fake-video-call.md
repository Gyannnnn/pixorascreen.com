## What Is This?

This is a fullscreen simulation of a video call: a grid of participant tiles, a control bar with mute and camera buttons, a connecting state while it "joins", and a call timer ticking along in the corner. It looks, from a few feet away or through a phone camera, like you are mid-meeting in a Zoom-style call. Nothing about it is real. No connection is made, no camera or microphone is touched, and your browser never asks for device permissions, because the page has no use for them.

People reach for it for three reasons. It makes a good **prank**, because a call is one of the few things nobody questions. It makes a useful **prop** for a skit or a thumbnail, where a real call would mean coordinating real people. And it is handy for **teaching and demos**, such as a lesson on video-call etiquette or a talk about remote work, where a live call would be unpredictable.

The interface is deliberately generic. It is our own design, not a copy of any particular product, so there are no real logos, names, or branding anywhere in it. That keeps it usable in a video you plan to publish, where reproducing a company's interface is the part that tends to cause problems.

## How to Use

### The freeze effect

This is the feature worth knowing about. Press the **Freeze** button or hit<kbd>B</kbd> and the call stalls: the tiles stop moving and go soft and washed out the way a stuck stream does, a buffering spinner appears with a "Reconnecting" message, and the call timer stops advancing. It is a toggle, so the frozen state holds for as long as you want and releases when you press it again. A frozen call is far more convincing than a perfect one, because everybody has sat through exactly that.

### Mute and camera

The microphone and camera buttons behave the way you would expect: both show a struck-through icon when off, a red dot appears beside your name when you are muted, and turning the camera off drops your own tile to a dark panel with your initials. Keyboard shortcuts are <kbd>M</kbd> and<kbd>C</kbd>.

### Participants

Choose four, six, or nine tiles under the call, and type your own comma separated names into the field then press Apply. The first tile is always you. Picking names your audience recognises is the single thing that makes the biggest difference to how convincing it looks.

### Fullscreen

Press <kbd>F</kbd> or the fullscreen button to lose the browser entirely, which is what sells it. <kbd>Esc</kbd> leaves. The Leave button ends the call to a "Call ended" screen with a Rejoin option, which is a tidy way to finish a bit.

## Use Cases

### Pranks and April Fools

Leave it fullscreen on a housemate's laptop and watch them wait for someone to speak. Freeze it just as they lean in. It is harmless and it resets the moment you close the tab.

### Video and social content

Skits about meeting culture more or less write themselves, and this saves you staging a real call to film one. Because the interface is original rather than a copy of a real product, it is safer to put in a video you intend to publish.

### Presentations about video-call etiquette

If you are running a session on remote meeting habits, a live call on the screen behind you is a better prop than a screenshot. The freeze effect gives you the frozen-participant moment on cue.

### A running joke, not an alibi

Pretending to be stuck in yet another meeting is a joke people have been making for years, and the screen works best as exactly that: a joke among people who will find it funny. It is not meant for convincing an employer, a teacher, or a client that you were somewhere you were not.
