## Warm, Low Light for Cozy Rooms

Most color screens are bright. Brown is different: `#7A4A24` is a dark, warm chestnut, so a fullscreen brown gives a soft glow even at full brightness. That makes it one of the most comfortable colors to leave running in the evening.

- **Film nights:** a second screen or a TV in a corner in brown gives the room a gentle, amber light that does not reflect harshly on the main screen.
- **Winding down:** a dimmed brown on a tablet by the bed works as a low night light with very little blue in it.
- **Cafés and shop windows:** a brown screen behind a counter or in a window display matches wood, leather, and coffee tones.

For a flickering warm light instead of a still color, the [fireplace screen](tool:fireplace-screen) adds animated flames and crackling sound.

## Backdrops for Photos and Video

Brown flatters warm subjects. Use a fullscreen brown behind small products such as leather goods, chocolate, coffee beans, wooden crafts, and baked goods, or as a muted backdrop for a vintage or sepia-toned shoot. Because brown is dark, it also keeps the attention on a brightly lit subject in front of it. The Download button saves the same shade as a PNG for editing or as a plain wallpaper.

## Checking How Your Display Handles Dark Warm Tones

Bright primaries are good for finding dead pixels, but they say little about how a screen handles the darker, warmer tones that make up skin shadows, wood grain, and night scenes in films. Brown is a good test of that.

1. Press <kbd>F</kbd> for fullscreen in a dim room.
2. Look for patches where the brown turns greenish, reddish, or blotchy. Those point to uneven color in the darker range, common on budget panels.
3. Type `5C371B` into the HEX field, then `3D2412`. A good screen shows each as a distinct, darker brown; if they collapse into near-black, the screen is crushing shadow detail.

If the steps blend together, try your monitor's gamma or black-level settings, or compare with the near-black values suggested on the [custom color screen](tool:custom-color-screen). The [grey screen](tool:grey-screen) checks the same midtone range without any color.

## Choosing a Shade

Brown ranges from pale tan to dark espresso. Raise the lightness in the HSL field for beige and tan tones, or lower it for coffee and chocolate. Moving the hue toward 20 makes the brown redder, like mahogany; moving it toward 40 makes it more golden, like oak. Save the shades you use with the Favorite button so they are ready next time.

If brown is too dark for the light you need, the [orange screen](tool:orange-screen) gives a brighter amber.
